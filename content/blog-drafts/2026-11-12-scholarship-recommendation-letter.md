A good **scholarship recommendation letter** can lift an application that is already strong, and a weak one can quietly sink it. Selection panels read hundreds of files. When your grades and essay look similar to the next candidate's, the letters often decide who moves forward.

The good news is that you control more of this than you think. You cannot write the letter yourself, and you should never try to. But you can choose the right person, ask in the right way and make their job easy. This guide shows you how.

## What do scholarship panels want from a recommendation letter?

Panels use letters to learn what your transcript cannot show: how you think, how you work with others and how you behave when things get hard. They look for:

- **Specific examples**, not general praise. "She redesigned our lab's data process and saved the team two days a week" beats "She is hardworking."
- **Evidence the writer knows you well**, such as how long and in what role.
- **A fair comparison**, such as "one of the top three students I have taught in ten years."
- **A link to your plan**: why you are ready for this particular programme.

A letter full of adjectives and no stories reads as a favour. A letter with two or three real stories reads as a recommendation.

## Who should write your recommendation letter?

Choose people who have seen your work closely, not people with the grandest titles. A lecturer who supervised your project and remembers details will write a better letter than a famous professor who barely knows you.

Good choices include:

- **A professor or supervisor** who taught or supervised you, especially for academic scholarships.
- **A workplace manager** if you have work experience, especially for leadership or professional programmes.
- **A community or volunteer leader** who watched you take responsibility, if the scholarship values service.

Avoid family members, friends and anyone who cannot speak about your work. Always read the call: many programmes say how many letters they want and whether they must be academic.

## When should you ask?

Ask **at least four to six weeks before the deadline**, and earlier if you can. Busy referees need time to write something thoughtful, and some institutions need extra days for a signature, letterhead or stamp.

Many applications also require letters to be recent, so check the call before asking someone to reuse an old one. Applying through an online portal? Your referee may get an email link and must complete it themselves, so tell them to watch for it, including the spam folder.

## How do you ask for a recommendation letter?

Ask politely, in writing, and give the person an easy way to say no. A referee who is lukewarm will write a lukewarm letter, so a "no" is better than a half-hearted "yes."

Here is a simple structure for your email:

1. **Greet them and remind them who you are**, including the course or project you worked on together.
2. **Name the scholarship**, the deadline and what it funds.
3. **Ask directly** whether they feel able to write a strong letter for you.
4. **Offer materials** and say when you will send them.
5. **Thank them**, whatever the answer.

## What should you give your referee?

Make their work easy. Send one tidy package:

- [ ] Your CV
- [ ] Your draft personal statement or statement of purpose
- [ ] A short description of the scholarship and what the panel values
- [ ] The deadline and how the letter must be submitted (portal link, email, signed PDF)
- [ ] A short list of the projects, grades or achievements you worked on together
- [ ] Any required form or template

A short reminder of specific moments, such as a presentation you gave or a problem you solved, helps your referee write real stories. You are not telling them what to say. You are refreshing their memory.

## What if a letter must follow a template or be signed?

Some programmes ask for letters on official letterhead, with a signature, date and stamp, or on a form they provide. Send the template early and ask your referee to check it. A missing stamp or an unsigned page can get an otherwise good file rejected.

If your referee writes in another language, check whether the programme wants a translation. If so, plan for the extra time.

## What mistakes should you avoid?

- **Asking too late.** A rushed letter is a generic letter.
- **Choosing a title over a relationship.** Specific beats famous.
- **Using the same letter everywhere.** Different scholarships value different things; give each referee the details.
- **Writing the letter yourself and asking for a signature.** This is dishonest, and many panels spot it. If you are ever asked to do this, say no politely.
- **Forgetting to follow up.** A gentle reminder one week before the deadline is normal and appreciated.
- **Skipping the thank-you.** Tell your referee the outcome. They will remember you, and you may need them again.

## Do you need to see the letter?

Often you cannot, and many forms ask you to waive your right to see it. That is normal and tells the panel the letter is candid. This is why choosing someone you trust matters so much.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many recommendation letters do I need?

It depends on the scholarship. Two is common, but always follow the call exactly.

### Can I ask a former teacher if I graduated years ago?

Yes, if they remember you and can give detail. Send a reminder of your work together along with your current plans.

### What if my referee says no?

Thank them and move on quickly. Ask someone else straight away, because time is the real danger.

### Should a letter be long?

One page is enough for most programmes. Clear, specific and honest matters more than length.
